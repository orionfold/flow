# When to escalate, and to whom

Most support questions are answered by the person who picks them up. This document is about the ones that are not, and its purpose is to make escalation a **decision with a rule** rather than a judgement call made under pressure.

## The rule

Escalate when **any one** of these is true. Not when it feels bad — when one of these is true.

1. **Data is at risk.** Anything a customer might lose. This escalates immediately and to a person, never to a queue.
2. **The same issue has appeared three times this week.** Three is a pattern, and a pattern is an engineering problem wearing a support costume.
3. **The customer has asked twice.** Regardless of the issue's size. Asking twice means the first answer did not land.
4. **You have spent 40 minutes.** Not because 40 is magic, but because the fourth 10-minute attempt is almost never the one that works.

## Who, for what

| Situation | Goes to | Response expected |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Data at risk | On-call engineer, directly | 15 minutes |
| Pattern of three | Engineering triage | Same day |
| Asked twice | Support lead | 2 hours |
| Billing dispute | Finance | Next business day |
| Anything about a contract | Account owner | Next business day |

## What escalating is not

It is **not** handing the problem over. The person who escalates stays on the thread and remains the customer's contact — the escalation adds a person, it does not swap one out. A customer who has to re-explain their problem to a second person has been escalated *at*, not helped.

> Every escalation gets a one-line note in the weekly review: what happened, which rule fired, and whether the rule was right. That last column is how this document changes.
