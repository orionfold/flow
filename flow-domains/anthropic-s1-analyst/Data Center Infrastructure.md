#### Data Center Infrastructure

Even where chips and electrical power are available, the development of data center facilities requires substantial time, capital, and specialized expertise. Data center construction involves lengthy permitting processes, complex supply chains for specialized equipment, and significant lead times for the installation and commissioning of cooling systems, electrical infrastructure, and networking equipment. Labor shortages, including shortages of, and competition for, electricians, facility technicians, and other skilled tradespeople, supply chain disruptions, permitting delays, and other factors can extend construction timelines and increase costs. Further, given data center development projects require advanced and significant capital commitments with dedicated end users, we have in some cases relied, and expect to continue to rely, on arrangements for financing or with partners who guarantee the lease obligations to support the development of data center capacity that is expected to host such partner’s chips or computing infrastructure. In addition, there are a limited number of qualified third-party vendors with the expertise we require and that support the particular infrastructure we wish to implement. Accordingly, we rely on this limited number of third parties for the construction, management, and operation of certain of our data center facilities, some of which have limited operating history or may have less experience operating data center infrastructure at the scale and complexity our operations require. In certain cases, these third-party operators are trained or supervised by our chip partners to manage facilities on our behalf but are not affiliates of those partners and may not have the same depth of expertise, operational resilience, or resources. Any failure by these third-party operators to perform as expected could disrupt our access to compute. In addition, safety or environmental incidents at facilities built or operated for us, whatever their cause, could expose us to claims, fines, or suspensions of construction or operations, the costs of which we may bear. We also depend on an extended network of suppliers, builders, and facility operators. If any of them engage in practices that are unsafe, unlawful, or contrary to the labor, human rights, environmental, or other standards we or our partners require, we could face legal liability, lose the ability to use affected suppliers or facilities, and suffer harm to our reputation, even where we did not direct or know of the conduct.

Our collective data center infrastructure is currently concentrated primarily in the United States, which exposes us to risks associated with regional regulatory changes, power grid constraints or failures, disruptions to network connectivity between facilities, natural disasters, physical attacks—including those motivated by geopolitical events, opposition to AI, and opposition to data center development—and other disruptions that could affect multiple facilities. Certain state, local, and municipal jurisdictions in the United States are also considering or have approved moratoriums on new data center construction, citing public health, safety, and environmental concerns. Such jurisdictions and other regulators, grid operators, or utilities may also adopt other measures that subject users of large electricity loads, including data centers, to curtailment, interruptions, forced reductions, disconnection, or other restrictions during periods of grid stress, whether through changes in

law or policy, market or reliability constraints, limits on transmission and distribution infrastructure, or a preference for protecting other utility customers and the broader grid. New large loads on the electrical grid may be increasingly subject to mandatory curtailment in regions where there is insufficient grid-connected power generation to reliably supply existing and new electricity loads. For example, states such as New York and Texas have recently moved to regulate large-scale data centers, whether by pausing state permitting for new large facilities, as New York has, or by subjecting large electricity loads to curtailment or disconnection during grid emergencies, as Texas has. Such moratoriums, or similar actions, could delay or prevent the construction or expansion of facilities on which we depend for compute. Opposition to data center development has also become more organized and coordinated across jurisdictions and our public profile may draw particular attention to projects associated with us. The loss of, or a significant disruption at, any major facility may not be readily absorbed by our remaining or future infrastructure. We are expanding our data center capacity internationally as part of our efforts to diversify our infrastructure footprint and reduce concentration risks associated with operating a significant portion of our compute infrastructure in the United States and to support our international growth strategy, including by entering into letters of intent and negotiating lease and related agreements for data center facilities in multiple non-U.S. jurisdictions, which exposes us to additional risks related to foreign regulatory requirements and approvals, data sovereignty and localization laws, governmental access or assistance requirements, cross-border data transfer restrictions, foreign tax regimes, and increased operational complexity. Our ability to enter into definitive agreements for data center capacity and operations in non-U.S. jurisdictions depends on the timely formation, registration, and receipt of governmental approval of local subsidiaries, as well as the establishment of local banking arrangements, and any delay or failure in completing these steps could delay or prevent us from mitigating our infrastructure concentration risks, executing our international growth strategy, securing planned compute capacity, or could result in substantial additional taxes, penalties, or other liabilities. In addition, regulatory, legal, and other constraints in many jurisdictions may limit the locations in which we can practically site or contract for data center capacity, which could constrain our ability to diversify our infrastructure footprint as quickly or as broadly as we currently intend.

We and our principal competitors rely on substantially similar inputs to develop and deploy frontier models, including advanced chips sourced from a small number of suppliers and data center capacity and compute obtained from CSPs and other third parties. As we and our competitors increasingly pursue vertical integration, the basis of competition could shift over time to encompass not only which company can develop the most capable models, but also which company can develop the best underlying components. Our future competitiveness may depend in part on our ability to successfully develop vertically integrated compute infrastructure on competitive terms and timelines, and there can be no assurance that we will be able to do so. Our competitors may be better positioned, or may invest greater efforts, to vertically integrate across the compute supply chain, including through proprietary chip design, fabrication, ownership or long-term control of data center infrastructure, access to dedicated power generation assets, exclusive supply arrangements with hardware manufacturers, or by securing the limited sites suitable for large-scale data center development ahead of us. Competitors that succeed in controlling a greater share of these supply chain layers may be able to secure compute at greater scale, lower cost, or with greater reliability than we can, particularly during periods of high demand or supply constraint, when captive supply relationships may confer certain advantages over participants that depend on third-party providers. Such competitors may also develop proprietary hardware- software co-designs that optimize compute efficiency for their specific model architectures, which could compound any capability gap across successive model versions. While we are pursuing initiatives to develop dedicated data center infrastructure, there can be no assurance that these efforts will be completed on the timelines or at the costs we anticipate, or that they will be sufficient to offset any structural advantages that vertically integrated competitors may achieve. Competitors that succeed in these efforts may be better positioned to remain at the frontier of AI regardless of broader market constraints.

Natural hazards, extreme weather, or other disruptions affecting the regions where these inputs are produced or where our facilities are located could further constrain the availability of chips, power, water, or data center capacity on which we depend. If we are unable to manage the foregoing risks, our business, financial condition, and results of operations could be adversely affected.
