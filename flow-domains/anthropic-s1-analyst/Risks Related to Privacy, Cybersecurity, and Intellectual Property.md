## Risks Related to Privacy, Cybersecurity, and Intellectual Property

The application of certain copyright law doctrines and contractual interpretations of open- source licenses to modern AI model training are uncertain.

The application of certain copyright law doctrines and contractual interpretations of open-source licenses to modern AI model training has not been authoritatively resolved by U.S. and non-U.S. courts as of this date. We have a good faith belief that we own or possess all sufficient legal rights and/or defenses to the training data used in or with our AI models, platform, and applications, which is based in part on our belief that our use or acquisition of the training data used in or with our AI models, platform, and applications constitutes “fair use” of such training data under copyright law doctrine. In addition, copyright laws in some jurisdictions outside the United States generally do not recognize fair use, and our reliance on analogous exceptions is subject to evolving conditions and to adverse or unsettled judicial interpretation in those jurisdictions. If a court of law disagrees with our belief that our use or acquisition of such training data constitutes fair use or otherwise concludes that it violates copyright law, for example, by adopting the reasoning in Bartz (as defined below), including without limitation in currently active litigation against us or another company in our industry, our business may be adversely affected. In particular, adverse rulings in litigation involving other AI developers, regardless of the specific facts, data practices, or legal theories at issue in those cases, could establish legal precedents or interpretations of copyright law that apply broadly across the industry and limit our ability to train, develop, or deploy AI models, even if our own data procurement and usage practices differ. Such outcomes could restrict access to certain categories of training data, narrow the scope of available legal defenses, or impose new licensing, compliance, or operational requirements across the AI industry. For example, if we cannot rely on the fair use doctrine to access copyrighted work for training our models, we may be required to pay significant fees to obtain a license to such training data or our ability to further develop our

In addition, an adverse ruling against us or another company in our industry could result in injunctive relief that restricts our ability to use or distribute models trained on the data at issue or could require us to retrain affected models from scratch at substantial cost. If we are unable to manage the foregoing risks, our business, financial condition, and results of operations could be adversely affected.

We are subject to complex and evolving laws, regulations, and other legal obligations related to privacy and data protection, and any actual or perceived failure to comply with such obligations could adversely affect our business, financial condition, and results of operations.

We are subject to a range of laws and regulations in the EU, the United States, and other jurisdictions, and other legal obligations relating to privacy, data protection, and the collection, use, transfer, storage, and other processing of data. These laws, regulations, and other legal obligations vary across jurisdictions, and include comprehensive frameworks such as the General Data Protection Regulation 2016/679 (“GDPR”), laws that impose sector-specific obligations, and those that impose specific requirements or restrictions on the handling of particular types of data, such as personal data that is considered sensitive. These obligations are not harmonized, continue to evolve in scope, present technical and operational complexities and challenges, and may conflict or impose overlapping or inconsistent obligations. Further, these laws and regulations, as well as other laws and regulations not originally enacted to address privacy or data protection, continue to evolve as they are applied to new technologies, including AI, and may be interpreted or enforced in ways that are inconsistent, unclear, novel, or subject to significant regulatory discretion. Additionally, plaintiffs, class action counsel, state Attorneys’ General, and other claimants continue to pursue claims against artificial intelligence companies relating to data handling, privacy, and cybersecurity; similar disputes could arise against us and they could take the form of privacy, cybersecurity, breach of contract, tort, or other claims. New or evolving laws, regulations, regulatory interpretations, claims, and novel or inconsistent application of existing laws and regulations to us, or our evolving contractual or other legal obligations, including those that arise as we seek to enter into new markets or product offerings, may require us to modify our models, platform, and applications, policies, and practices, delay or limit the development or deployment of certain features, increase compliance and litigation costs, and result in fines or penalties.

Our compliance obligations and expenditures, and our exposure thereunder, may vary depending on both how customers access and use our models, platform, and applications, including through our first-party offerings or third-party distribution channels, and on the nature of data processed in connection with those offerings. For example, our costs and regulatory exposure may vary based on the demands we receive from individuals, regulators, and other parties seeking to exercise rights under privacy or data protection laws. We may be subject to additional obligations, restrictions, or exposure in connection with the processing of data that is considered sensitive under applicable laws. In connection with the development, deployment, and improvement of our services, including model training, improvement, and inference, we have faced, and may face in the future, operational errors or claims or assertions that our use or processing of data, such as personal, sensitive, or confidential data, is inconsistent with our obligations.

Failure to secure data against risks to its confidentiality, integrity, or availability may result in legal obligations, such as notification obligations to individuals, regulators, or customers, which could result in additional legal and reputational risk.

Any actual or perceived failure or inability by us, our personnel, or third parties acting on our behalf to comply with applicable privacy or data protection requirements, our contractual commitments, or our public statements regarding our data practices could result in investigations, litigation, fines, penalties, or other liability; require us to change our models, platform, and applications, policies, or practices; reduce customer demand or harm our reputation; require us to make public disclosures that could expose us to reputational and legal risks; and otherwise adversely affect our business, financial condition, and results of operations.

We process personal and sensitive data for AI safety, and any actual or perceived failure to comply with the legal and regulatory obligations applicable to such processing could adversely affect our business, financial condition, and results of operations.

Any security breaches or other incidents, including those that affect our networks, systems, or data, or the networks, systems, or data of our service providers, suppliers, or customers, could adversely affect our business, financial condition, and results of operations.

We store, handle, and otherwise process confidential, proprietary, and personal information, and our customers use our models, platform, and applications to process similarly sensitive information. We experience security risks, unintended exposure of or access to data, cyberattacks, and other attempts to gain unauthorized access to our information, sites, networks, systems, models, platform, and applications. These have resulted in, and may in the future result in, breaches of our security measures or those of our third-party service providers; unauthorized access to our sites, networks, systems, and accounts; unauthorized access to, and misappropriation of, personal, confidential, or proprietary information belonging to us, our customers, or others; viruses, worms, spyware, or other malware being served from our models, platform, and applications, networks, or systems; deletion or modification of content; the display of unauthorized content on our models, platform, and applications; interruption, disruption, or malfunction of operations; and other risks.

We have in the past faced and in the future may face risks of phishing schemes and other cyberattacks, including by nation-states and state-sponsored actors, that continue to evolve in sophistication and volume, and may inherently be difficult to detect. Our proprietary model weights, corporate and financial data, and other core intellectual property represent particularly high-value targets for state-sponsored espionage and theft, and security researchers have identified frontier AI companies as priority targets for non-U.S. intelligence services. The efforts of such state-sponsored actors to target us will likely increase over time, especially as we develop models with next-generation capabilities, and they may specifically target models which have not, or have not yet, been released to the general public. For example, in light of its advanced cyber vulnerability discovery capabilities, state-sponsored actors may conduct cyberattacks specifically targeting Mythos.

Our security capabilities continue to evolve, and certain aspects of our security program may not be sufficient to fully address the most sophisticated malicious actors, including well-resourced nation-state or state-sponsored groups, especially as those groups develop or obtain more powerful technology, which may include AI technology with offensive capabilities similar to those displayed by Mythos. While we are actively investing in and enhancing our security infrastructure and controls, we have operated in the past, and may in the future operate, for periods of time with known limitations or gaps in our defenses as we continue to mature our security posture, and no information security program can be expected to prevent all possible incidents. There can be no assurances that we will be able to prevent or adequately detect and defend against all cyberattacks or other cybersecurity incidents affecting our systems, including incidents caused by nation-state and state- sponsored actors. An unauthorized exfiltration of our model weights could enable adversaries to bypass significant research investment, eliminate key competitive advantages, and potentially raise national security concerns if such technology is acquired by malicious actors. Further, if our model weights were obtained by unauthorized actors, such actors may be able to deploy such models outside of our usage policies, safety mechanisms, or monitoring frameworks, which could enable harmful or misuse scenarios that our safeguards are designed to prevent and could subject us to regulatory, reputational, or public scrutiny for having developed such capabilities.

We have faced and expect to face insider threat risks and vulnerabilities related to hiring processes, identity verification, remote work environments, and compromised devices that could enable unauthorized access to sensitive systems or information. We have been and expect that we will continue to be targeted by individuals seeking employment under false identities, or with ulterior or malicious motives for seeking employment with us and the third parties on which we rely. We also face the risk that employees, contractors, or other personnel

We have also seen, and expect to continue to see, industry-wide software supply chain vulnerabilities and attacks, which could affect our or third parties’ systems, or the security of our models, platform, and applications. Geopolitical tensions or armed conflicts around the world may increase these risks. As a result, we may be unable to prevent or timely detect all attempts to access or exfiltrate sensitive information, including by actors with substantial technical, financial, or geopolitical resources.

As we continue to develop and deploy new features, including those incorporating AI, our systems are exposed to additional or evolving security risks, including those arising from emerging or imperfectly understood AI- specific threat vectors. Such AI-specific threats include prompt injection attacks that manipulate model behavior through malicious inputs, attempts to replicate or reverse engineer our proprietary models through systematic API queries, adversarial inputs designed to cause model failures or misclassifications, malicious corruption of training data that could compromise model integrity, and vulnerabilities in continuous integration and deployment pipelines that could allow insiders or other actors to inject malicious code that is subsequently propagated or amplified through AI-assisted development workflows. Such attacks may be difficult to detect, may not be addressed by traditional cybersecurity defenses, and may require specialized expertise, techniques, and infrastructure to mitigate. Further, features that rely on AI may be particularly susceptible to unanticipated vulnerabilities as both our understanding and the broader market’s understanding of AI-related security risks and mitigation techniques continue to develop. Our efforts to mitigate the foregoing risks may result in false positives and our mistakenly limiting or blocking certain users from accessing our technology, which could result in potential litigation or regulatory action.

In addition, our agentic offerings interact with external tools, APIs, databases, code repositories, and other third-party systems, creating cybersecurity attack surfaces that are distinct from those applicable to traditional AI models. An adversary could manipulate the content of an external data source, API response, document, or web page processed by an agent to cause the agent to take harmful or unauthorized actions based on that manipulated input. An attacker could also attempt to compromise an active agent session and redirect the agent to perform malicious actions using the customer’s authenticated credentials and permissions. Because our agentic offerings are designed to take autonomous actions with real-world consequences, including executing code, modifying files, sending communications, and completing transactions, a successful exploitation of these agentic attack surfaces could result in data exfiltration, unauthorized transactions, code injection, or other adverse effects, potentially across multiple customer deployments simultaneously. Traditional cybersecurity defenses, including firewalls, input validation, and access controls, may not be fully effective against these novel threat vectors, and the security community’s understanding of agentic-specific attack surfaces is still developing.

Such issues have resulted and may in the future result in service disruptions, degraded performance, unintended or harmful outputs, or the unauthorized disclosure of personal or proprietary information, including through security incidents that impact the networks, systems, and data of our customers. Although we have dedicated significant resources designed to identify and address these security risks, we cannot guarantee that our security measures will be effective or that our recovery protocols, backup, and other systems will be sufficient. Even where we detect an incident, our response may prove inadequate, including if we misjudge its severity, respond too slowly, or fail to fully remediate the underlying cause. In addition, incidents that appear minor when they occur may later prove significant when considered together with other incidents or with information learned afterward, and a series of related incidents, none of which is material on its own, could prove material in the aggregate. We continue to devote significant resources to protect against security breaches and other incidents, and we may need to devote significant resources in the future to mitigate and address problems caused by breaches and other incidents, which may divert resources from the growth and expansion of our business.

Any actual or perceived security breach or other incident experienced by us or third parties with whom we do business, particularly our CSPs, could damage our reputation and brand, cause our business to suffer, require us to expend significant capital and other resources, cause us to incur increasing costs, including costs to deploy additional personnel and protection technologies, train employees, and engage third-party experts and consultants, expose us to litigation or regulatory action, cause us to violate applicable privacy, data protection, security, and other laws and regulations, subject us to adverse publicity and a loss of confidence in our security measures, and cause us to incur significant fines, penalties, and other liability exposure, which could adversely affect our business, financial condition, and results of operations. We have taken steps to limit our liability for certain incidents, for example through warranty disclaimers and limitations of liability in our terms of service, but those contractual protections may not be upheld, or may not apply to certain claims, or may otherwise be insufficient to limit our liability exposure under applicable law.

In the EU, we face increasingly stringent regulatory reporting obligations for cybersecurity and AI-related incidents. Under the GDPR, we must notify the competent regulators about incidents that involve personal data and are likely to result in a risk to the rights and freedoms of individuals. Similarly, the EU Network and Information Security Directive 2022/2555 (“NIS2”) requires us to notify the competent authorities of any significant cybersecurity incident through a staged process. In addition, the EU AI Act requires providers of general-purpose AI models with systemic risk to keep track of, document, and report serious incidents without undue delay to the AI Office and competent authorities. Going forward, providers and deployers of high-risk AI systems, as defined under the EU AI Act, will also have to report serious incidents or malfunctions without undue delay in certain circumstances.

In the United States, companies are increasingly subject to analogous incident reporting obligations, including under federal and state data breach notification laws, such as the New York Stop Hacks and Improve Electronic Data Security (SHIELD) Act. In addition, certain U.S. states have adopted incident reporting laws specific to frontier model providers, such as the New York Responsible AI Safety and Education Act (RAISE Act) and the California Transparency in Frontier Artificial Intelligence Act. These frameworks create heightened compliance and liability exposure in case of delayed, incomplete, or inaccurate incident disclosures.
