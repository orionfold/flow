#### Electrical Power and Fuel Constraints

The operation of data centers at the scale required for frontier AI development and deployment consumes substantial quantities of electrical power. The availability of sufficient electrical power has emerged as a significant constraint on data center development and expansion in many geographies, including the United States. Electrical grid infrastructure in the United States and other countries was not designed to accommodate the rapid increase in demand associated with AI development, and the lead times required to build new generation capacity, transmission infrastructure, and grid interconnections can extend for years. Demand on electric grid infrastructure is also increasing as a result of the increased electrification of homes, vehicles, and industry, and this growing demand is occurring at a time when power generation capacity additions, transmission expansion, and grid modernization efforts have not kept pace and certain existing generation resources are being retired, all of which has contributed to higher electricity prices and longer interconnection timelines in certain regions. We have publicly committed to covering the cost of electricity price increases to consumers caused by our data centers, including both the cost of electricity itself and the infrastructure necessary to deliver it.

Our access to electrical power also depends on our ability, and the ability of the third parties on which we rely for the construction, management, and operation of our data center facilities, to negotiate and maintain contracts with utility providers and grid operators and to obtain approvals from public utility commissions and other regulators. These parties may be unable or unwilling to approve, or may condition or delay, the connections, capacity expansions, special or negotiated rate arrangements, and infrastructure investments necessary to support data center growth, or may grant them only at costs that limit the economic efficiency and viability of certain facilities, such as by requiring us to bear grid upgrade or interconnection costs beyond those attributable to serving our facilities—representing a larger share of costs than is required under existing or prior regulatory constructs. Interconnection queues for large new electricity loads have also lengthened substantially in many regions, which could delay or limit our access to grid power. Utility providers and regulators may also reallocate previously committed capacity that our data center facilities may require. In many jurisdictions, a single utility provider has substantial control over the terms, timing, and cost of the service and infrastructure necessary to power our data center facilities. The commercial and regulatory priorities of utility providers may differ from ours, including with respect to which infrastructure investments are made, how their costs are allocated, and whether alternative or self-supplied generation arrangements are permitted and on what conditions. Utility providers and other stakeholders may also seek regulatory or legislative measures affecting large electricity loads, self-supply, or alternative power generation arrangements. The outcomes of our negotiations and regulatory proceedings involving utilities are uncertain, and unfavorable results could increase our costs, delay our projects, or limit the alternatives available to us. In addition, the construction of the generation and transmission infrastructure on which we depend is itself capital-intensive and exposed to labor shortages, supply chain disruptions, permitting delays, changes in interest rates, tariffs, other cost overruns, and financing constraints. The significant and increasing capital required to fund these generation, transmission, and related infrastructure investments, whether incurred by us, our utility and energy supply providers, or our data center development partners, is sensitive to prevailing interest rates and the availability of debt or equity financing on acceptable terms. Any inability of our partners to access capital when needed, and the limitations, cost, and competition for such capital, could increase the cost of the power on which we depend, delay or halt projects, or render certain facilities commercially unviable, and under certain of our arrangements, we bear these financing and capital costs directly or on a pass-through basis. Furthermore, newer sources of generation, such as solar, wind, battery storage, nuclear, and geothermal power, may not be available in the quantities or on the timelines we require, or may be available only at higher cost. Our facilities separately depend on access to reliable water for cooling, which in some regions is scarce, subject to use restrictions, or increasingly costly. Any of the foregoing could adversely affect the availability of the power or water we require, increase our costs, or limit our ability to develop, expand, or operate our data center facilities, which could adversely affect our business, financial condition, and results of operations.

Competition for access to electrical supply and data center locations with adequate power availability is intense, and we compete for such access with well-capitalized CSPs, some of which produce and develop competing AI models, cryptocurrency mining operations, and other large-scale electricity consumers. In addition to, or sometimes in lieu of, grid-based electricity, an increasing share of data centers are being developed with dedicated on-site power generation capacity that supplies electricity directly to the facility independent of any utility-operated electric grid. The reliability of such off-grid power generation solutions varies by the underlying technology, the equipment used, and the operator and will reflect site-by-site operational and environmental profiles. Development of this new capacity in turn requires power generation and electrical infrastructure that are themselves subject to supply challenges and operational complexity. For example, certain of our planned data center facilities rely on dedicated on-site natural gas-fired generation, which exposes us to fuel price volatility and supply disruptions that are outside of our control and for which we bear the cost on a pass- through basis. These facilities are also subject to constraints on pipeline capacity and firm fuel transportation necessary to deliver natural gas. As data center operators increasingly rely on natural gas-fired generation, and because behind-the-meter and on-site arrangements are feasible only in some jurisdictions given the applicable regulatory frameworks, we expect increased competition among users for the same natural gas supply and pipeline capacity in those areas. This competition could drive up the price of natural gas in such areas and may adversely affect our ability to contract for firm fuel supply and transportation and to secure the pipeline connections and laterals our facilities require.

These dynamics present several related risks. We may be unable to secure firm gas supply and transportation during the development of a facility, causing a planned site to be delayed or abandoned; we may face fuel supply or delivery disruptions once a facility is operating; and because our arrangements generally provide for fuel and related costs to be borne by us on a pass-through basis without cost caps, we remain exposed to increases in the cost of natural gas and its transportation. More broadly, the cost of electricity is subject to significant variability that is largely outside of our control, as wholesale electricity prices fluctuate with changing supply and demand, weather, fuel prices, tariffs or surcharges, and grid stress, and can rise sharply during periods of peak demand or constrained supply, and such increases in energy costs could adversely affect our results of operations.

In some regions, including certain regions where we have contracted compute commitments, data center development has prompted public controversy and regulatory scrutiny regarding environmental impact, land and water use, and the allocation of limited power resources. For example, if our efforts to secure electrical power are perceived to affect other utility customers and the broader grid, regulators may impose conditions, standby charges, or other requirements intended to address such impacts. More broadly, our access to electrical power is subject to an evolving regulatory framework characterized by complexity, jurisdictional overlap, and rules that were largely designed around service by incumbent utility providers and existing generation arrangements and that may not readily accommodate new entrants or alternative approaches, which can result

in significant delay or prevent the development of the generation we require. Grid-tied electrical supply, for instance, is subject to the jurisdiction of federal and state energy regulators, regional grid operators, and public utility commissions, while behind-the-meter and on-site generation is subject to a distinct set of exclusive service franchise protections, permitting, environmental, air quality, interconnection, and siting requirements. In addition, environmental standards applicable to power generation continue to evolve, including as a result of changing governmental priorities and shifting public and political views regarding climate change and the appropriate treatment of various power generation sources. Any failure to secure access to adequate electrical power, any legal, regulatory, or other administrative action or change limiting the availability of electricity, or any significant increase in energy or compliance costs, could adversely affect our cost structure and limit our ability to scale our compute infrastructure, develop and deploy our models, and service our customers. We have also made, and may in the future make, voluntary commitments to offset the impact of our data center energy consumption on local communities and electricity ratepayers, including commitments to cover grid infrastructure upgrade costs, procure new power generation to match our energy needs, invest in curtailment systems to reduce strain on the grid during peak demand periods, and address environmental impacts. These commitments have increased, and we expect will continue to increase, our operating costs. Moreover, we believe this is part of a broader emerging trend in the AI industry, and such commitments, or similar obligations, may become increasingly standard or required, which could further increase our costs. In addition, the physical effects of climate change may increase our operating costs over time, as higher average temperatures can increase the power consumption and water usage required to cool equipment at the facilities on which we depend.
