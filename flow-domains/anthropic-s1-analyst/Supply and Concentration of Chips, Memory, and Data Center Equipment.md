#### Supply and Concentration of Chips, Memory, and Data Center Equipment

The specialized chips required for AI workloads, particularly high-performance AI accelerators and memory, are produced by a limited number of manufacturers. The production of advanced chips is extraordinarily complex, requiring manufacturing processes at the leading edge of technological capability.

The majority of the world’s most advanced chip manufacturing capacity is concentrated in Taiwan, primarily at Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company (“TSMC”). This geographic concentration creates significant risks, including exposure to natural disasters such as earthquakes and typhoons, potential disruption from geopolitical tensions between Taiwan and China, vulnerability to any interruption in Taiwan’s power grid, water supply, or other critical infrastructure, and susceptibility to developing trade regulations and restrictions. Any military conflict, tariffs and trade restrictions, economic coercion, blockade, natural disaster, or other incident affecting Taiwan could severely disrupt the global supply of advanced chips, with effects that could persist for years given the time required to construct and qualify alternative fabrication facilities. Efforts to establish sufficient advanced chip manufacturing capacity outside of Taiwan, including in the United States, remain in early stages and may not produce adequate compute to materially reduce these concentration risks within the foreseeable future. In addition, due to the demand for advanced chip manufacturing, TSMC has, at times, been unable to fully satisfy market demand for chip manufacturing capacity, and as a result, we have not always been able to obtain sufficient quantities of chips on a timely basis. Accordingly, any restrictions or delays on the supply of advanced chips out of Taiwan or TSMC would adversely affect our business, financial condition, and results of operations.

In addition, both high bandwidth memory, which is a key component in high performance AI accelerators, and other high performance memory products used in compute infrastructure, are largely manufactured by SK hynix Inc., Samsung Electronics Co., and Micron Technology, Inc. Each of these manufacturers is working to expand its production capacity to meet demand. However, there is no guarantee that these efforts will result in adequate supply fast enough to meet demand, particularly if large customers or competitors secure exclusive supply commitments or otherwise reserve substantially all of a manufacturer’s available production capacity.

The concentration and constraints in manufacturing supply expose us to the risk that manufacturers may make allocation decisions or strategic shifts that adversely affect our access to the advanced chips needed for our business. We also face risks that specialized chips may contain defects, or that their production or delivery may be delayed or disrupted, which, given long replacement lead times, could constrain available compute and adversely affect our operations and ability to scale. A meaningful portion of the components we and our partners rely on is sourced from or manufactured outside of the United States, and new or increased tariffs, import restrictions, or trade disputes could delay deliveries, force supply-chain changes, or raise our costs, some of which we bear directly under pass-through arrangements. For more information, see the section titled “—Governmental trade controls, including export and import controls, sanctions, customs requirements, and related regimes, could subject us to liability or loss of contracting privileges or limit our ability to compete in certain markets.”

We also depend on third-party chip design and services companies for the design, manufacture, and support of certain custom accelerator platforms, as well as for related infrastructure financing arrangements. Disruption to these relationships, or any failure by these parties to deliver hardware or services on expected timelines and specifications, could adversely affect the availability, performance, or cost of accelerator platforms on which we rely.

Data centers also require electrical, generation, and transmission equipment, cooling systems, backup generators, and networking hardware, some of which are made by only a few manufacturers, must be ordered years in advance, and require substantial pre-delivery payments that we may not recover if our plans change. In addition, certain equipment, such as high-voltage transformers, switchgear, turbines, generators, substations, and transmission components, has been subject to extended delivery timelines, price increases, tariffs and national security requirements, and allocation constraints as demand from data center operators and other large electricity consumers has grown. If such equipment is delayed or unavailable, we may be able to replace it

only at higher prices or under less favorable long-term commitments. In order to secure near-term access to compute, we may also elect, or be required, to procure long-lead-time generation and electrical equipment at prices above prevailing market rates on the expectation that it can be deployed on an accelerated timeline to unlock capacity in the near term. Realizing the benefit of paying such prices depends on the timely and successful engineering, procurement, and construction of the associated facilities, which requires specialized expertise that is currently in short supply and involves significant execution risk. Delays, cost overruns, or failure to secure energy on the anticipated schedule could mean that we do not obtain the energy and capacity for which we paid such prices. In some cases, these arrangements also depend on a developer or other counterparty agreeing to purchase the equipment at such prices, finance such purchase, and recover those costs and associated financing costs from us over time, and we may bear these costs even if the underlying capacity is delayed or never realized. Our equipment supply framework agreements and similar arrangements may expose us to further risks, including minimum purchase or take-or-pay commitments, price escalation, and obligations that we are unable to align with our actual deployment needs.

Additionally, the U.S. government has imposed and may continue to impose export controls restricting the sale of advanced chips and chip manufacturing equipment to certain countries, including China. These restrictions have affected global chip and related equipment supply allocation, and future changes in export control policy could affect the availability or pricing of the compute resources on which we rely. Future export controls could be more restrictive, for example, by placing restrictions on the countries in which advanced chips may be installed or in which model weights may be stored, which could limit where we or our partners are able to build data centers, constrain our international expansion, or increase our costs.
