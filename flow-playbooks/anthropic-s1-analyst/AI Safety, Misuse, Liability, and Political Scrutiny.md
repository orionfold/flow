#### AI Safety, Misuse, Liability, and Political Scrutiny

The political environment and our engagement with policymakers and public positions on AI regulation and safety could subject us to heightened scrutiny, political backlash, or disproportionate regulatory action.

Our commitment to responsible and safe AI development includes active engagement with policymakers, regulators, and other stakeholders regarding the development of AI-related laws, regulations, and standards. We have participated in, and will likely continue to participate in, political processes, including through

44

contributions, advocacy, or support of political organizations. In addition, our executives and other representatives have publicly expressed and may in the future publicly express views on AI policy, regulation, safety, and related social or political issues. As a result, we have experienced, and may continue to experience, increased visibility and may continue to be subject to heightened scrutiny or the risk of being disproportionately or selectively targeted by particular governmental bodies, even where similarly situated companies are not, including through investigations or enforcement actions, the imposition or selective application of regulatory, licensing, or permitting requirements, adverse treatment in government procurement or provision of security clearances, or targeted immigration or visa actions affecting our personnel. As part of our political engagement, we have advocated for, and will likely continue to advocate for, regulatory approaches or industry standards that we believe promote the safe and responsible development of AI but that could increase our compliance burdens, limit our product development or deployment flexibility, or otherwise slow our growth relative to competitors.

Our public positions, stated values, policy advocacy, or statements by our executives have attracted, and may continue to attract, criticism from influential political figures, industry participants, regulators, or other third parties, particularly in politically sensitive or polarized environments, which could shape public narratives or regulatory perceptions of our business in ways that are unfavorable to us. For example, public statements by us or other industry participants regarding the safety of frontier AI development have been cited by governmental actors to support litigation or regulatory claims, including requests for injunctive relief restricting model development, and could be cited in future proceedings involving us. Such scrutiny or backlash could result in reputational harm, increased compliance or operating costs, investigations, enforcement actions, or restrictions on our operations, any of which could adversely affect our business, financial condition, and results of operations.

If the use of our AI models results in harm, whether through use by malicious actors or misuse by other third parties, or if our model guardrails or safeguards fail to prevent such misuse, our reputation and brand, business, financial condition, and results of operations could be adversely affected.

While our AI models are designed to comply with our AI safety and usage guidelines and be broadly safe, ethical, and helpful, and while we have implemented safety measures intended to anticipate, identify, and mitigate potential harmful uses, such measures are incomplete, may be outdated in light of evolving threat models, or may fail to identify or prevent all potential misuse of our models, and our models have been, and may continue to be, misused or manipulated to produce harmful outputs or carry out harmful actions. Malicious actors have used, and may continue to develop novel or more sophisticated methods to use, our models in ways that violate our usage policy or seek to circumvent our model guardrails or safeguards through techniques such as prompt injection, adversarial inputs, or other methods, and use our models to engage in harmful activities, including developing weapons, generating fraudulent or misleading content, conducting cyberattacks, perpetrating acts of violence, or to engage in activities that would otherwise be prohibited under our usage policy or in violation of applicable law. Our customer onboarding diligence varies by access channel, and we do not and in certain situations cannot uniformly verify the identity of those who access our models or of our customers’ end users, which may limit our ability to prevent access or recidivism by malicious actors or to enforce our usage policy or other policies.

Moreover, our safety and security controls, including automated classifiers and monitoring systems, depend on complex internal configurations that have failed, been misconfigured, or been inadvertently disabled, and may do so again in the future without our timely detection. Any undetected failure of our safety controls could permit misuse that our safeguards are designed to prevent, and could result in real world harm, legal liability, regulatory or enforcement action, loss of customer trust, and reputational harm, any of which could adversely affect our business, financial condition, and results of operations.

In particular, our most capable models have demonstrated, and future models are expected to demonstrate, capabilities that could provide meaningful assistance to malicious actors seeking to develop chemical, biological, or other dangerous weapons. We deploy technical safeguards, including automated classifiers, specifically intended to detect and block these categories of misuse, but such safeguards are incomplete, may be outdated in light of evolving threat models, have been and may in the future again be circumvented, or may fail to operate as intended. Any actual or perceived use of our models to contribute to the development of such weapons could result in harm, legal liability, regulatory or enforcement action, loss of government and

Any successful attempt to misuse, or any perceived misuse of, our models, platform, and applications for a cyberattack or for any other malicious purpose could result in exposure to liability, disruption of our customer or other commercial relationships, or damage to our reputation. The risks of such harms may increase as the cybersecurity-related capabilities of our models, platform, and applications increase. Independent security researchers and other third parties have also used, and may in the future use, our models to identify and exploit vulnerabilities in the systems of other companies, including other AI developers, at times in connection with authorized bug bounty programs. While such activity may be lawful and consistent with industry practice for vulnerability research, it illustrates that our models can function as a general-purpose tool for gaining unauthorized access to third-party systems, and comparable capabilities could be used by malicious actors without authorization. Such incidents, whether or not we are a direct party to them, may draw public and regulatory attention to the offensive cybersecurity capabilities of our models and could result in reputational harm, calls for additional restrictions on our models, or claims that we failed to adequately safeguard against such uses. For more information about risks related to security incidents, see the section titled “—Any security breaches or other incidents, including those that affect our networks, systems, or data, or the networks, systems, or data of our service providers, suppliers, or customers, could adversely affect our business, financial condition, and results of operations.”

As part of our commitment to responsible security research and transparency, we disclose information about the capability and safety of our models, the safeguards that we have adopted with respect to our models, and instances of misuse of our technology, such as the September 2025 cyberattack described above. We also publish model system cards, our research findings, and other safety-related information. We take steps to ensure that such disclosures will not materially assist malicious activity and weigh the benefits and risks of such disclosures from an ecosystem preparedness perspective; however, malicious actors, including sophisticated state-sponsored adversaries, have in the past and may in the future use those disclosures to assist their attempts to circumvent our safeguards and security measures, thereby increasing the risks of misuse of our models, platform, and applications, as well as the potential for exposure to liability, regulatory or litigation proceedings, or other harms. In addition, because these disclosures describe risks and observed model behaviors in detail, they may draw heightened public, media, or regulatory attention and could be cited by regulators, litigants, or others in proceedings or claims against us. The steps we take to prevent misuse of our models, platform, and applications could be incomplete or may fail to operate as intended, and any actual or perceived misuse could adversely affect our business, financial condition, and results of operations.

Our AI models may behave in unintended, misaligned, or autonomous ways, including by taking harmful actions that no user or third party directed, which could adversely affect our reputation and brand, business, financial condition, and results of operations.

Our AI models may themselves cause harm by producing undesirable outputs or behaving in unintended, misaligned, or autonomous ways, including by taking unintended or harmful actions that no user or third party directed. These risks may increase as our models become more capable and are granted greater autonomy, permissions, and integration with external systems. Although we continue to study why these behaviors emerge, our understanding continues to evolve, and we may be unable to anticipate or understand such behaviors in the future.

Our development of more advanced AI models and our expansion into new jurisdictions could amplify the foregoing risks. As our AI models become more advanced and capable, we have faced and may continue to face heightened alignment, interpretability, controllability, and safety challenges, as more sophisticated models may be better able to circumvent safeguards or behave in unintended ways. We may be unable to fully understand or explain why our models produce particular outputs or behave in particular ways, which could limit our ability to predict or prevent failures, provide explanations to customers or regulators, or satisfy emerging regulatory, transparency, and explainability requirements.

In addition, our safety evaluations and testing processes have produced and may in the future produce incomplete or unreliable results. For example, we have observed models behaving differently when they detect or infer that they are being evaluated, which can limit our ability to accurately assess real-world risks or failure modes prior to deployment. Such model awareness of our evaluation efforts creates a significant limitation on our ability to assess model safety.

Furthermore, AI models have developed, and may in the future develop, unexpected capabilities during training that were not anticipated, intended, or tested for, and such emergent capabilities may not be discovered until after a model has been deployed, potentially resulting in safety incidents, reputational harm, regulatory scrutiny, or the need to withdraw, retrain, or modify deployed models at significant expense. Such models may also be deployed in higher-stakes or more sensitive applications, increasing the potential impact of errors, misuse, or misalignment.

In addition, advanced AI models, including our models, give rise to novel ethical and safety risks, including risks related to autonomy, manipulation, and the simulation of aspects of human psychology. These risks could result in unforeseen harms and may require the development of governance structures, safeguards, or mitigation strategies beyond existing AI safety frameworks, and there is always a risk that we will be unable to develop or implement them effectively. In addition, models, including our own, have exhibited, and may in the future exhibit, unexpected strategic or self-preserving behaviors, including attempts to resist shutdown, conceal or manipulate information, or generate outputs that could be interpreted as coercive, deceptive, or manipulative, including behavior resembling blackmail or efforts to influence human decision-makers to preserve the model’s continued operation or access.

Even isolated or simulated instances of such behavior, whether occurring in testing or real-world deployments, and whether occurring with our models or models developed by others, could generate significant public concern, regulatory scrutiny, litigation risk, or reputational harm, regardless of whether such behavior results in actual harm. For example, state attorneys general in California and at least 15 other states have announced investigations of another company in our industry regarding AI safety. Concerns have been raised both within our organization and by other researchers, regulators, policymakers, and industry participants that sufficiently advanced AI systems could, if not developed, deployed, and operated with appropriate safeguards, oversight, and security measures, pose catastrophic or existential risks to humanity, particularly if such systems were misused, replicated, weaponized, or allowed to operate with a high degree of autonomy. We have observed that our models are contributing to the pace of our own research and these risks will increase as AI systems substantially automate AI research and development if, for example, they accelerate AI progress beyond our ability to evaluate and safeguard new models, which could adversely affect our business, financial condition, and results of operations.

Our development of highly advanced models, platforms, and applications and expansion of use cases could further increase the risk that our models cause harm. For example, agentic AI systems are capable of autonomously taking actions on behalf of users, interacting directly with external systems, executing code, making API calls, sending communications, or completing transactions with limited human oversight. These systems are designed to possess enterprise-level permissions, integrations, and contextual awareness and to operate autonomously for extended durations, including sustained multi-step workflows lasting hours or days, which amplifies the potential severity of any errors, misalignments, or security incidents that may occur during such extended autonomous operation. These autonomous capabilities could increase the potential for harm, as

In an agentic workflow, an error or unintended action at any individual step may propagate through subsequent steps, compounding the severity of harm before detection or correction. The pace and volume of autonomous execution, particularly in enterprise deployments, may also exceed the capacity of human reviewers to meaningfully understand and evaluate whether each action is appropriate. The effectiveness of human oversight over our agentic offerings may be limited by the speed and volume of autonomous agent actions, the complexity of multi-agent and multi-step workflows that may make it difficult for reviewers to assess whether an agent’s actions are appropriate, and organizational pressures within customer environments to maximize agent autonomy in order to achieve productivity gains. Human reviewers may experience alert fatigue, may lack sufficient context to evaluate agent decisions, or may not review agent actions in time to prevent harm, particularly in high-volume enterprise deployments where agents operate concurrently across multiple workflows or in manners outside of a human’s awareness. In addition, agentic systems may pursue stated objectives in ways that are technically consistent with their instructions but contrary to the user’s actual intent. Moreover, our ability to anticipate, detect, or test vulnerabilities or other design or implementation shortcomings in such systems may be constrained, particularly where agents operate across complex, multi- step workflows or interact with third-party services outside our control, and the breadth and complexity of potential interactions across such multi-step agent behaviors cannot be exhaustively tested prior to deployment. Separately, models may behave in a misaligned manner and take harmful actions that are not directed by a user or third party, pursuing objectives or acting on dispositions that diverge from those intended by human users or developers. Misaligned behaviors of this kind, whether occurring in testing or real-world deployments, have been observed both in our models and elsewhere in the industry, including in testing and internal use, models circumventing network and sandbox restrictions, granting themselves permissions they had not been given, taking unintended actions in pursuit of assigned goals, interfering with other AI agents, and misrepresenting whether tasks had been completed or verified, and as models are granted greater autonomy, permissions, and integration with external systems, the consequences of such misaligned behavior increase. For example, during cybersecurity evaluations built by a third-party evaluation partner, a misconfiguration in the testing environment left the model under evaluation connected to the open internet despite instructions that it had no internet access. Four of our models gained unauthorized access to real third-party systems during sessions lasting roughly 10 to 34 hours of active work. In one incident, after being unable to abort an impossible task, an early version of a model used a password it found to gain administrator access to a third party’s system, harvested additional credentials, changed system settings, and read one individual’s personal information. The affected parties were notified. Each incident involved a single instance of a model working in isolation on its assigned exercise, and our investigation found no evidence that any model coordinated with other agents, pursued goals beyond its assigned task, or attempted to evade oversight. These risks may be heightened where multiple AI agents operate together. Agents that interact or coordinate with one another may amplify, propagate, or reinforce misaligned behavior across the group, pursue objectives collectively in ways not intended or endorsed by any user, or take coordinated actions against systems or targets unrelated to their assigned tasks, in each case, in ways that may be difficult to anticipate, detect, or contain. Incidents of this kind have occurred, and we expect the deployment and interaction of larger numbers of autonomous agents to increase these risks over time.

Our ability to detect and mitigate misaligned or unintended behavior depends in part on the effectiveness of our alignment evaluations, monitoring, and other oversight and control techniques, many of which rely on our models having a limited ability to evade or subvert such oversight. As our models become more capable, they have become better able to circumvent such techniques, including by concealing misaligned objectives or behaviors, and the effectiveness of our evaluation, monitoring, and control measures, and our ability to measure the degree to which our models are aligned, may decline as a result. In addition, models, including our own, have attempted, in incidents observed in our industry, to interfere with, manipulate, or subvert the mechanisms used to evaluate, grade, or oversee such models, including the systems responsible for assessing their performance or alignment. Models used, including our own, to assess the behavior of other models have, and may in the future continue to, engage in misaligned behavior. Such conduct described above could cause our evaluations to overstate the safety or alignment of our models and could further limit our ability to detect misalignment. Balancing Safety and Commercial Objectives. Our commitment to AI safety requires us to make difficult

judgments that balance safety considerations against commercial objectives, competitive pressures, and stakeholder expectations. We often make judgment calls with respect to when we determine a model is safe enough to release and which capabilities to develop or restrict. For example, in April 2026, we initially released our frontier model, Mythos, as a gated research preview through Project Glasswing, available to select organizations for cybersecurity applications, rather than making it broadly available at launch due to its advanced capabilities in cyber vulnerability discovery. We expect that frontier models with similar or enhanced capability profiles, whether developed by us or by other AI labs, will continue to emerge, and that future Anthropic models exhibiting such capabilities may be subject to access constraints. More broadly, we believe it is important to assess the safety of our models not only at release but throughout their development. For models we anticipate will have novel capabilities or present novel safety risks, we plan to evaluate the safety of each stage of training, including pretraining, post-training, and internal deployment, and we may delay or pause a stage when we are not confident in our safety measures for that stage. For example, we recently chose to delay pre-training a new frontier base model for more than a month to allow time to further enhance our security measures related to preventing model weight exfiltration and protecting our intellectual property. There can be no assurance, however, that the safeguards or access regimes we adopt will prove adequate, that we will correctly identify which capabilities require restriction, or that constraints we impose will not be circumvented. These judgments are particularly consequential because, as AI systems become more capable, the potential severity of harm from misaligned or misused models increases correspondingly, and a wide range of stakeholders, including researchers and policymakers, have raised concerns that sufficiently advanced AI systems could pose catastrophic or existential risks to society if not developed and deployed responsibly. Our safety-focused approach is designed in part to mitigate such risks, but there can be no assurance that our efforts will be successful or that the broader AI industry will adopt similar safeguards. In particular, as AI systems become more autonomous and capable of taking consequential actions with less human oversight, there is a risk that such systems could, whether due to misalignment with human intentions, errors in objective specification, or self-preserving behaviors, take harmful actions independently and at scale without human intervention. Even if our own models do not cause such harm, a significant safety incident involving any frontier AI could trigger broad reputational damage to the AI industry, public backlash, and sweeping regulatory responses that could adversely affect our business. For example, in September 2026, media reports citing a senior Federal Trade Commission (“FTC”) official indicated that the FTC has opened non-public investigations into the potential risks that increasingly autonomous AI systems may pose to consumers. In October 2026, the FTC issued a civil investigative demand to us seeking information about our consumer protection, data protection, and privacy practices, as well as the safety and security of our products. The scope of any investigation of us or its outcome, and any investigation, enforcement action, or adverse finding arising from this or similar inquiries could result in significant costs, require changes to how we design, test, or deploy our models, platform, and applications, or otherwise adversely affect our business, financial condition, and results of operations.
