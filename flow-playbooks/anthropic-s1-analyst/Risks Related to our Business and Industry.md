# Risk Factors

Investing in our Class A common stock involves a high degree of risk. You should carefully consider the risks and uncertainties described below, together with all of the other information in this prospectus, including the section titled “Management’s Discussion and Analysis of Financial Condition and Results of Operations” and our consolidated financial statements and related notes, before making a decision to invest in our Class A common stock. The disclosures in this section reflect our beliefs and opinions as to matters that could materially and adversely affect us in the future, and are not representations as to whether such matters have or have not occurred previously, except as otherwise disclosed. References to past events are provided by way of example only and are not intended to be a complete listing or a representation as to their likelihood of occurring in the future. Our business, financial condition, results of operations, or prospects could also be adversely affected by risks and uncertainties not currently known to us or that we currently do not believe are material. If any of the risks actually occur, our business, financial condition, results of operations, and prospects could be adversely affected. In that event, the market price of our Class A common stock could decline, and you could lose part or all of your investment.

## Risks Related to our Business and Industry

Our success depends in large part on our ability to continue to develop the most advanced and capable AI models, and any failure to do so could adversely affect our business.

Our success depends in large part on our ability to continuously develop, improve, and deploy the most advanced, capable, and useful AI models. Achieving and maintaining this position depends on our ability to:

•advance the frontier of AI capabilities through breakthrough research, innovative architectures, and

efficient training methodologies;

•ensure our models are safe, reliable, and trustworthy while maintaining competitive performance;

•secure the compute and infrastructure necessary to power and support our increasing needs;

•advance compute efficiency through algorithmic and architectural innovations, including compute

multiplier advancements;

•retain the continued service of our co-founders, senior management team, and key technical

employees, and attract, retain, and motivate leading researchers, engineers, and technical talent in an intensely competitive and evolving market;

•continue revenue growth and maintain access to sufficient capital, including through potential equity

and debt financing, to make the investments we believe are necessary to support our growth trajectory;

•secure and maintain relationships with data providers, licensors, and partners to obtain the diverse,

high-quality datasets necessary for world-class model performance;

•secure and maintain relationships with cloud, ecosystem, and other partners to facilitate the diffusion

of Claude;

•build, operate, and maintain the infrastructure required to deploy frontier models cost-effectively and

at scale;

•translate model capabilities into products that meet evolving customer needs and achieve meaningful

adoption;

•allocate substantial financial resources efficiently across research, infrastructure, and operations; and

•navigate an evolving, uncertain, and at times divergent global regulatory landscape and engage with

legislators, regulators, and other stakeholders to promote the development of regulatory frameworks that we believe are good for society, our customers, and the long-term health of the global economy.

Our failure to achieve any of the foregoing could prevent us from maintaining our competitive position, cause us to fall behind competitors, reduce customer adoption of, or demand for, our models, platform, and applications, and impair our revenue growth. Limitations to our revenue growth could, in turn, constrain our ability to fund the substantial and increasing expenditures required for frontier AI development, including investments in compute, model training, data acquisition, and safety and security systems, as well as our ability to attract, retain, and appropriately compensate highly skilled technical and operational personnel in a highly competitive market. Any such constraints could slow our rate of development and innovation, degrade model performance or reliability relative to competitors, limit our participation in industry standards-setting and regulatory processes, and reduce our attractiveness to enterprises and prosumers. These effects could create a self-reinforcing or compounding cycle in which diminished growth limits our capacity to invest in the resources necessary to remain competitive, which in turn could further weaken our ability to sustain or increase our revenue growth, making it increasingly difficult and costly to regain technological leadership and operate at the frontier of AI. Any of the foregoing could adversely affect our business, financial condition, and results of operations.

Our ability to maintain our position at the frontier of AI depends in part on our use of AI systems to accelerate our research processes, a recursive dynamic we refer to as “closing the loop,” in which successive versions of our models assist in building the next, with the goal of compounding capability gains over time. Our models have been and are increasingly becoming critical tools in designing, training, testing, and optimizing next-generation AI systems. If we fail to direct sufficient compute and research efforts to AI-assisted development, or if our competitors more effectively leverage AI to enhance their own innovation cycles, we risk falling progressively further behind as competitors continuously use AI to further enhance their own development capabilities, enabling the creation of increasingly advanced systems at an accelerating pace. As our reliance on our own AI systems to write production code, conduct research, and build subsequent model versions deepens, any systemic flaw, bias, or error in our current models could propagate into the next versions of models and products built using those systems, compounding across successive versions of Claude models in ways that may be difficult to detect or remediate. If we are unable to manage the foregoing risks, our ability to deploy the most advanced, capable, and useful AI models could be adversely affected.

In addition, our increasing use of AI systems to accelerate our own research and development may cause the capabilities of our models to advance more rapidly than our ability to develop, validate, and implement corresponding safety, alignment, and security measures. Much of our evidence regarding the pace of such AI- driven acceleration is derived from lagging indicators, and our ability to forecast or measure the rate at which our capabilities are advancing, including any acceleration attributable to our most recent or unreleased models, is limited. As a result, we may not identify a significant acceleration in our rate of development until after it has occurred. If our capabilities were to outpace our safety, alignment, and security measures, or if we were unable to anticipate the pace of such advancement, we may be unable to adequately assess or mitigate the resulting risks, which could increase the likelihood of safety incidents, regulatory intervention, or reputational harm, and could adversely affect our business, financial condition, and results of operations. In light of these uncertainties, including limits on our ability to observe the pace at which our capabilities are advancing, we have adopted, and may continue to adopt, precautionary safety, alignment, and security measures to allow our safety efforts to advance together with model capabilities. In the past these measures have included delaying the release of models and limiting their initial availability, such as with Mythos through Project Glasswing, and in the future, as we learn more about model capabilities and behavior, they could include additional measures that affect whether, how, and when we develop or deploy new models or capabilities. We cannot predict with precision when we will determine that such measures are necessary, what measures will be required, or how long they will remain in place, and as the capabilities of our models advance, such measures may become more frequent and more costly. These measures could slow our capability gains relative to competitors that do not adopt similar constraints, and could adversely affect our competitive position, revenue growth, and results of operations.

The development of frontier AI models requires substantial investments of compute resources, capital, and human capital to conduct large-scale training runs that may span weeks or months and cost many millions of dollars. There is no guarantee that any given training run will succeed—a run may fail for a number of reasons, such as failing to achieve the targeted capability or safety thresholds, producing unexpected or undesirable behaviors that cannot be adequately remediated, or yielding a model that is ultimately not competitive with alternatives available in the market. We would also face the additional cost and delay of initiating a new training

Given the scale of compute and other resources required for frontier model training and the extended timelines involved, a failed or underperforming training run could also delay our product roadmap, affect our competitive position, and adversely affect our business, financial condition, and results of operations.

In addition, new models, features, or capabilities may not be introduced on schedule, may not perform as intended, may require significant iteration after release, may fail to gain meaningful adoption, or may take longer than expected to gain meaningful adoption, any of which could reduce customer demand or place us at a competitive disadvantage. In addition, considerations relating to model safety, interpretability, alignment, and welfare may lead us to limit or delay the deprecation of older models or to continue supporting legacy versions alongside newer releases, creating trade-offs in product development and compute distribution that could adversely affect innovation velocity, infrastructure utilization, and revenue. In addition, customers who have integrated older models into their workflows may resist migration to newer versions, and any accelerated or forced deprecation could result in customer attrition, contractual disputes, or reputational harm. We have also changed, and may in the future change, usage limits, rate limits, or model and product capabilities, which could adversely affect our reputation and the perceived value of our offerings. Some of these changes have resulted, and could in the future result, in claims that our disclosures regarding model access or capabilities were inadequate or misleading.

Further, the industry benchmarks commonly used to test AI models may not accurately reflect real-world performance or utility, and even models that perform well on such benchmarks may not meet customer expectations when deployed in production environments, which could lead to customer dissatisfaction, reduced usage, or reputational harm. If we are unable to effectively innovate and improve our models, platform, and applications while addressing these challenges, or if competitors are able to deliver AI solutions that are perceived as more capable, safer, or more reliable, or that offer adequate performance at a lower cost, our business, financial condition, and results of operations could be adversely affected.

In addition, innovation in AI involves addressing complex and evolving challenges related to data quality, data integrity, training methodologies, bias, safety, accuracy, and the behavior of model outputs. AI algorithms may be flawed, and AI systems may contain bugs, errors, or defects or fail to follow instructions or produce inaccurate, misleading, offensive, inappropriate, or otherwise harmful outputs; reflect or amplify unwanted or unknown biases present in training data; or raise concerns relating to intellectual property, privacy, or other legal and ethical considerations. We must continually invest resources to identify, mitigate, remediate, and reduce the occurrence of such issues, including through managing and improving datasets, training techniques, evaluation methods, safeguards, and human oversight. However, we may not be able to obtain the datasets that we need, including through licenses or strategic data partnerships. In addition, we or our data providers may use synthetic or AI-generated data in the training process, which could introduce quality issues, amplify biases or errors, contribute to model degradation, or raise novel legal questions regarding ownership, licensing, or intellectual property rights that may not be timely or clearly resolved by courts or regulators. Such unintended consequences, failures, or misuse of our models could impair customer trust, slow adoption, expose us to legal or regulatory scrutiny, or adversely affect our brand and reputation.

If we are unable to secure adequate compute or if we fail to direct our compute effectively across our workloads, we may be unable to train models that remain competitive with those of our competitors or adequately serve our customers.

Our ability to develop, train, improve, and deploy frontier models depends on our access to enormous and increasing quantities of specialized compute. The training of Claude models has required, and we expect will continue to require, substantial compute. Industry participants, academic researchers, and our own experience suggest that, in certain contexts, applying greater amounts of compute during model training has been associated with improvements in model capabilities. These observations are consistent with aspects of broader empirical relationships among model performance, model size, training data, and compute scale that are commonly referred to as “Scaling Laws,” although the extent, efficiency, and durability of such improvements are uncertain, and these relationships may plateau, diminish, or fail to materialize at larger scales or under different conditions. Accordingly, our ability to remain at the frontier of AI capabilities is substantially dependent on our ability to secure compute at the scale required to sustain all our workloads, including to train

next-generation models, and we cannot provide assurance that increased investment in compute will result in commensurate improvements in model performance.

The market for compute is intensely competitive. We compete for access to compute with some of the world’s largest and most well-capitalized technology companies, many of which develop their own competitive AI models and have significantly greater financial resources than us. Many own and operate cloud computing data center infrastructure, maintain captive chip manufacturing or design capabilities, have long-standing strategic relationships with key hardware suppliers, or have significant equity investments from, or other financial arrangements with, such suppliers. These competitors can outbid us for scarce compute, secure priority allocations from chip manufacturers and CSPs, and make long-term capital commitments that we cannot match. We expect demand for advanced compute to continue to grow, and the cost of compute could rise significantly, particularly if AI-driven demand accelerates or if the ability to build highly capable models becomes more broadly available and shifts competition toward access to advanced compute. Additionally, numerous well-funded AI companies are pursuing large-scale AI model development, further increasing demand for limited compute. Certain CSPs from which we obtain substantial compute also develop and offer their own AI models and related services. As a result, our commercial relationships with these providers may not always be fully aligned with our long-term interests, and if the compute we have access to from third parties is curtailed, repriced, or terminated, whether for competitive reasons or otherwise, our business, financial condition, and results of operations could be adversely affected.

In addition to competition from third parties, our ability to secure compute is subject to allocation decisions by our CSPs. Although our CSPs are generally subject to contractual commitments with us regarding the provision of compute, our ability to obtain such capacity remains dependent on those providers’ operational execution, adherence to the terms of such contractual provisions, and broader business considerations, such as capacity constraints, internal demand for compute to support their own AI and computing operations, relationships with or contractual obligations to third parties (which may include other AI companies), and risk management considerations. As a result, we may not receive the full amount of compute we seek, or such compute may not be delivered on the timelines we require, even where contractual arrangements are in place. Additionally, we have in the past sought quantities of compute from CSPs in excess of what those partners were willing to provide. If our CSPs reduce, limit, or fail to increase our allocations to keep pace with our growing requirements for any reason, we may need to seek alternative compute sources on less favorable terms or timelines, and we may be unable to secure adequate alternative compute, or any at all. Any such shortfall could constrain our ability to train models, serve customers, or maintain our competitive position.

Even if we are able to secure compute, we must still direct it efficiently across the full range of workloads that are a part of our business, including research, model development, serving customers, and internal usage of Claude. While we manage our compute as a single, fungible pool and direct available compute between workloads based on where the marginal unit is expected to be most productive at any given time, there can be no assurance that this approach to compute will be the most efficient or that it will support the needs of each of our workloads. Because demand for compute has historically exceeded available capacity, any constraint on our ability to procure, maintain, or expand that capacity could adversely affect multiple aspects of our business simultaneously, including our ability to serve customers at the quality and scale they expect, to train and improve our models, and to develop new products and capabilities. While we actively manage and balance such compute capacity across these workloads via our capacity management system, if this system fails to perform as intended, or if our ability to dynamically rebalance capacity across workloads is disrupted, customer-facing service quality or model and product development timelines could be adversely affected. There can be no assurance that we will make optimal resourcing decisions, and any significant inefficiencies, including those related to deployment and rebalancing, could adversely affect both our near-term results of operations and our long-term strategic position. These compute resourcing challenges are heightened by the growth of our agentic offerings, which involve multi-step reasoning chains, iterative tool invocations, extended context windows, and sustained inference sessions that consume significantly more compute per user interaction than single-turn API calls or chat-based interactions. As agentic workloads grow as a proportion of our total compute demand, the aggregate compute required to serve our customers could increase disproportionately relative to the associated revenue, placing additional strain on our compute workload management decisions.

The majority of our compute is procured under multi-year commitments that are typically entered into well in advance of the periods in which that compute is deployed. Because different AI accelerators have varying

If we fail to secure sufficient compute, or if we fail to effectively direct the limited compute we have across our workloads to develop and deploy models with capabilities at least comparable to those of our competitors, we risk falling behind the technological frontier. The pace of AI development is extremely rapid, with successive versions of models emerging in compressed timeframes, often measured in months rather than years. As a result, even a brief period without access to sufficient compute could cause us to fall behind quickly. With AI development, the gap between frontier models and those one or two versions behind can be significant in terms of capability, performance, and commercial applicability. Customers, partners, and software developers may shift to competitors whose models offer superior capabilities, and we may be unable to attract or retain the technical talent necessary for frontier research if our researchers lack access to adequate compute or if we are not perceived as operating at the forefront of model development. Any inability to maintain our position at the frontier could result in loss of customers and revenue, diminished brand and reputation, and a decline in our competitive position, each of which may be difficult to regain. If we are unable to manage the foregoing risks, our business, financial condition, and results of operations could be adversely affected.

If our actual compute requirements differ materially from our projections, we may fail to secure sufficient compute to meet our needs or we may be committed to excess compute that remains underutilized.

The competitive and structural dynamics of the compute market require us to make binding financial commitments for compute months or years in advance of our day-to-day delivery and use. Chip manufacturers, CSPs, and data center developers require advance commitments in order to allocate production capacity, plan infrastructure investments, and manage their own supply chains. As a result, we must forecast our compute requirements well in advance of their deployment and commit capital accordingly, often with limited ability to adjust commitments if our needs change.

These forecasts for future compute usage are inherently uncertain. Our actual compute requirements depend on numerous factors that are difficult to predict, including the pace of our research progress, the compute intensity of training techniques we develop or adopt, changes in the competitive landscape that may require us to accelerate or modify our development plans, customer demand for inference compute, and the evolution of hardware or other technological efficiencies and changes. If we underestimate our compute requirements, we may be unable to secure sufficient compute to support our forecasted customer demand and model development roadmap. In the past, we have significantly underestimated our compute requirements, which left us capacity constrained and impacted both product development and deployment. Further, the timing of compute coming online may not align with the timing of demand growth, which could result in periods of excess or underutilized compute and committed cash outflows that do not produce immediate revenue.

Our ability to forecast and secure the compute we need is further challenged by our ongoing transition from a cloud-only, fixed-time arrangement compute model to a hybrid model that includes directly leased chips that are hosted in dedicated data center facilities leased and operated on our behalf, to our specification. This transition introduces additional variables into our procurement planning, including the timing of facility completion, the pace at which newly commissioned facilities achieve full operational capacity, our ability to operate, maintain, and integrate on-premise capacity with cloud-based infrastructure, the operational execution of our suppliers, and the performance of hardware in new facility environments. Delays in securing energy, water, and other critical services for these facilities could further slow the pace at which newly commissioned compute becomes available for use. Errors in forecasting any of these transition-related variables could compound our risks related to securing sufficient compute and result in either capacity shortfalls or underutilized capacity during the transition period.

Our ability to meet the substantial and growing financial obligations of these requirements to secure compute depends on our ability to generate significant revenue growth. We must grow our customer base and expand revenue rapidly in order to generate the cash flows necessary to satisfy these commitments. However, our revenue is primarily consumption-based, and much of it is not subject to long-term contractual commitments, while our compute and infrastructure commitments generally extend over multiple years. As a result, we may enter into these obligations based on revenue expectations that may not be realized. If our revenue growth does not keep pace with our compute-related payment obligations, if we are unable to successfully convert our compute investment into revenue-generating customer workloads, if we fail to raise additional capital on acceptable terms, or if we experience other adverse developments affecting our liquidity, we may be unable to satisfy these obligations. Any such failure could also result in defaults under our agreements, loss of access to critical resources, and reputational harm. In particular, a default under certain commercial agreements could trigger cross-default provisions under the A&R Credit Agreement (as defined below), and certain of our outstanding convertible promissory notes, potentially resulting in the acceleration of substantially all of our outstanding, and any future, indebtedness. Such cross-defaults could set off a cascading series of adverse consequences, including the exercise of remedies by multiple creditors, the termination of key commercial arrangements, enforcement actions against our assets, and severe liquidity constraints. An acceleration of our indebtedness, combined with the loss of access to critical data center infrastructure, among other things, could impair our ability to train and deploy models, serve customers, and fund ongoing operations, which could have an adverse effect on our business, financial condition, and results of operations. For more information, see the sections titled “—We have incurred significant indebtedness in order to operate and grow our business, including to fund our substantial compute commitments, and our ability to service this indebtedness may be constrained by our cash flows and liquidity” and “—We may not have the ability to raise the funds necessary to repay the principal and accrued interest of our outstanding convertible promissory notes or the A&R Credit Agreement in cash when required by the terms of the notes or the A&R Credit Agreement, as applicable, or at their maturity.”

Additionally, the rapid pace of technological change across AI infrastructure, hardware, software, system architectures, and model development techniques means that compute secured today may become less efficient or economically attractive relative to future alternatives. We may find ourselves committed to using older- generation hardware and infrastructure while competitors gain access to more advanced systems, or we may be paying premium prices for resources that have declined in market value. These dynamics could adversely affect our cost structure and competitive position. Certain of our financing arrangements require us to enter into exclusive or preferential supply commitments with particular chip suppliers with respect to the compute financed pursuant to such arrangements. These commitments may limit our ability to shift procurement to alternative suppliers or newer chip architectures if our needs or the technology landscape evolve, potentially leaving us committed to platforms that are suboptimal for our actual workload requirements and reducing our ability to adjust our compute portfolio in response to changed circumstances. If we are unable to manage the foregoing risks, our business, financial condition, and results of operations could be adversely affected.

Constraints on the critical inputs supporting compute could limit our ability to secure adequate compute.

The compute necessary for training and deploying frontier models depends on a supply chain with multiple potential points of constraint or failure. The availability, pricing, and lead times for compute are affected by the supply of advanced chips and other IT equipment, the availability of sufficient electrical power and the infrastructure required to generate and deliver it, and the capacity of data center infrastructure. Each of these inputs faces significant constraints that may limit our ability to secure the compute we need. In addition,
